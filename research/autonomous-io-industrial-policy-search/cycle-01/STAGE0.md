# Cycle 1 — Stage 0: Research Question & Primitive Audit

## 1. One-sentence question

When a public buyer procures a complementary innovation system, can bundled versus separate interoperable lots change complementor entry and post-deployment concentration, and when is unbundling welfare-improving despite coordination and scope losses?

## 2. Motivating institution

Public procurement rules allow a contracting authority to choose whether a contract is divided into lots, to specify whether bidders may bid for one or several lots, and to limit how many lots one tenderer may win. Innovation procurement is explicitly treated as an industrial-policy tool.

The motivating institution is therefore generic public procurement for innovative systems, not Triangle Ehime and not a local legal form.

## 3. General economic primitive

A principal procures two complementary tasks or components. A supplier may be able to perform both tasks, while specialist suppliers can perform one task. A bundled award gives scope/coordination benefits; separate lots can admit more firms and may change the market structure after deployment.

The post-award complementor market is a theoretical extension, not a fact established by the procurement rule.

## 4. Candidate welfare distortion

- Bundling can reduce procurement cost or protect task coordination, but may exclude specialists and concentrate future supply.
- Lotting can increase entry and contestability, but may lose economies of scope, create interface failures, or facilitate bid coordination.
- A public buyer may value future competition and innovation that a procurement winner does not internalize.

## 5. Strategic actors

- Government / public buyer: chooses bundle versus separate lots and possibly a cap on awards.
- Integrated or broad-scope supplier: chooses whether and how to bid for the bundle.
- Specialist complementors: decide entry and bids for individual lots.
- Private downstream buyers are passive in the minimal candidate; their post-deployment demand is a welfare channel, not a required strategic type.

## 6. Government instrument

Contract architecture: bundled award, separate lots, optional multi-lot bidding, and award caps. This is a genuine policy/design choice, not a firm-specific transfer.

## 7. Nearest theory families

- Procurement bundling and combinatorial auctions.
- Public-private partnership task bundling.
- Procurement with sequential tasks and scope externalities.
- Lot division, entry, SME access, and bid rigging.
- Dynamic procurement for technology acquisition and future competition.
- Vertical scope and contract design.

## 8. Prohibited rescue

The branch may not be rescued by adding:

- deployment data ownership or data sharing;
- network effects, standards, or interoperability as a new primitive;
- local-content or regional eligibility;
- coalition transfers, core stability, or joint-liability payments;
- post-award licensing, patents, or platform governance;
- a second policy instrument chosen only to manufacture a new result.

## 9. Fatal null hypothesis

Once the system is represented as a principal choosing bundled versus separate procurement of complementary tasks with scope economies, information, and entry, the proposed result is a relabeled procurement-bundling or contract-scope result. Any post-deployment concentration result is an application implication rather than a new strategic mechanism.

## Stage 0 decision

**GO TO STAGE 1.** The question has a real policy instrument, a plausible welfare wedge, and a clear kill test. The novelty burden is high because the primitive is close to mature procurement-contract theory.
