# Cycle 05 — Stage 0: Research Question and Primitive Audit

## 1. Candidate question

Can a credible advance public purchase commitment for an unproven technology
induce initial supplier capacity investment while deterring later supplier
entry when the government re-tenders, thereby changing the future supplier
structure?

## 2. Motivating institution

Forward Commitment Procurement (FCP) and related public procurement of
innovation practices give suppliers advance information about a public need
and may provide an agreement to purchase a product or service that does not
yet exist, conditional on future performance and cost. This is a demand-pull
institution, not a local-government proper noun.

Primary institutional sources:

- Finance Northern Ireland, *Innovation in Public Procurement* (2017):
  https://www.finance-ni.gov.uk/sites/default/files/publications/dfp/PGN-02-17-Innovation-in-Public-Procurement.PDF
- OECD, *Public procurement for public sector innovation* (2024):
  https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/public-procurement-for-public-sector-innovation_9aad76b7-en.html
- OECD, *Public Procurement, Trade and Industrial Policies* (2026):
  https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2026/06/public-procurement-trade-and-industrial-policies_e9b7ed1d.html

## 3. General economic primitive

One public buyer can commit at date 0 to a future purchase opportunity for a
currently unavailable technology. A supplier makes an irreversible
capacity/product-development investment before the technology market is
contested. At a later date, the buyer can either renew/award to the initial
supplier or re-tender, and potential suppliers decide whether to enter.

The primitive is therefore:

[
\text{credible future demand} + \text{irreversible supplier investment}
+ \text{later procurement competition}.
]

The institutional commitment is translated into a dynamic buyer commitment
choice, not assumed to be a legal right in every procurement regime.

## 4. Candidate welfare distortion

The commitment may correct an ex ante market-pull/underinvestment problem.
The same commitment may create:

- incumbent capacity or lock-in that deters efficient later entry;
- an excessive initial investment race;
- buyer hold-up or inability to select a lower-cost entrant later;
- a trade-off between innovation creation and future competition.

The welfare question is whether the demand guarantee changes both investment and
the number/identity of future suppliers, rather than merely raising output.

## 5. Strategic actors

- Government/public buyer: chooses whether and how credibly to commit.
- Initial supplier: chooses irreversible capacity or development investment.
- Potential entrant(s): decide whether to invest/enter before the later
  procurement contest.

For minimality, the formal candidate would use one incumbent and one entrant,
with the government as a policy-moving buyer.

## 6. Government instrument

One instrument: advance purchase commitment strength/credibility, followed by a
specified future procurement date. The benchmark is no commitment. A later
re-tender is part of the procurement timing, not a second policy instrument.

## 7. Nearest known theory families

- Dynamic procurement and incomplete contracts.
- Procurement competition with investment and underinvestment.
- Short versus long-term procurement contracts.
- Supplier competition, capacity investment, and sourcing design.
- Innovation procurement / demand-pull industrial policy.
- Dynamic entry, lock-in, and hold-up.

## 8. Prohibited rescue

Do not add a second subsidy, public pilot signal, data-sharing rule,
interoperability mandate, regional spillover, multiple buyer, coalition,
platform, vertical-integration choice, or non-replicable public asset after
the literature gate. Do not turn a commitment into a new primitive by
renaming a standard long-term contract.

## 9. Fatal null hypothesis

A standard two-period procurement model with buyer commitment, supplier
investment, and later competition already generates the claimed investment
and entry effects. If so, the FCP application contributes no distinct
industrial-organization mechanism.

## 10. Stage-0 decision

**GO TO STAGE 1.**

The question is economically important and has a clear policy-induced market
structure wedge. Its prior-art risk is high, so it must face an aggressive
Stage-1 and Stage-2 kill test.
