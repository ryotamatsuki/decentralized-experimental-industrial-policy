# Q3 Stage 3 — Candidate Mechanisms

Date: 2026-08-28

Frozen question:

> How should technology-search and matching responsibilities be divided between local and national innovation intermediaries when the value of intermediation depends jointly on adopter-side problem articulation and supplier-side market thickness?

Stage-2 binding rule: Q3 must not reduce to local information versus central scale, a standard matching platform, generic search frictions, generic platform integration, or a private-platform model with government labels.

## M1 — Split-side investment complementarity

**Mechanism.** Local intermediaries invest in adopter onboarding/problem articulation while a national intermediary invests in supplier recruitment; each side's participation raises returns to the other side's intermediation effort.

**Loop.** local articulation → adopter participation → supplier return to national recruitment → supplier participation → adopter return to articulation.

**Endogenous margins.** adopter participation/search; supplier participation/search; intermediary effort allocation.

**Minimum players/timing.** local intermediary, national intermediary, adopters, suppliers; two intermediation efforts then participation.

**Minimum new primitive.** task-specific intermediation effort.

**Closest threat.** two-sided platform investment/network-effect models, especially Tan–Anderson–Parker (2020), plus platform-governance models.

**Candidate result.** split governance may underprovide complementary investments.

**Welfare.** underinvestment in platform/intermediation quality.

**Institutional interpretation.** local problem-discovery teams plus a national supplier-recruitment hub.

**Fatal attack.** This is just complementary platform investment allocated across two owners.

**Status.** `KILL`.

## M2 — Demand legibility × supplier thickness

**Mechanism.** Local problem articulation converts tacit needs into a legible/searchable challenge; greater legibility changes suppliers' willingness to incur search/entry costs into a national pool, while supplier thickness changes adopters' return to articulating/submitting problems.

**Loop.** articulation/legibility → supplier entry/search → thickness → adopter submission → return to articulation.

**Endogenous margins.** articulation intensity or breadth; supplier participation; adopter participation.

**Minimum players/timing.** local intermediary, national hub, suppliers, adopter/problem owner.

**Minimum new primitive.** a legibility/translation technology linking problem formulation to supplier search.

**Closest threat.** Brancaccio–Kang (2025) product design/search frictions; Zhong (2022) platform search precision; Kanoria–Saban search design; PPI demand-articulation literature.

**Candidate result.** an interior split architecture might dominate if local translation is required before national pooling.

**Welfare.** too little or too much problem standardization/searchability.

**Institutional interpretation.** turning a local business problem into a challenge brief usable by a broad supplier market.

**Fatal attack.** Once written minimally, this becomes local task quality plus platform search-design/standardization. Avoiding that requires an additional contracting or information mechanism.

**Status.** `KILL AFTER DEEP DIVE`.

## M3 — Selective referral and adverse national-pool composition

**Mechanism.** Local intermediaries keep easy/high-fit problems in local channels and refer harder/niche problems to a national supplier hub; suppliers anticipate the composition of referred problems and decide whether to participate, so selective referral can thin the hub and reinforce local retention.

**Loop.** local referral threshold → composition of national problem pool → supplier participation/thickness → expected value of referral → local referral threshold.

**Endogenous margins.** local referral/escalation; supplier entry/search; national-pool composition.

**Minimum players/timing.** multiple local intermediaries, suppliers, national hub; local problem types may be represented by exogenous easy/hard cases; locals route then suppliers participate.

**Minimum new primitive.** a local outside channel that makes some problems more attractive to retain than refer.

**Closest threat.** Bolton–Santos–Scheinkman (2016) cream-skimming; Romanyuk–Smolin (2019) cream-skimming in matching; Idem (2023) centralized/decentralized market coexistence; Arbatskaya–Konishi (2012) referrals; Rust–Hall (2003) competing intermediation modes.

**Candidate result.** a split hierarchy could create a thin-hub trap in which the centralized pool receives adversely selected problems and loses supplier participation.

**Welfare.** split governance can be worse than pooling if local routing ignores its effect on national participation.

**Institutional interpretation.** local agencies solve the easy cases themselves and escalate only difficult cases to a national technology broker.

**Fatal attack.** The strategic core is adverse selection/cream-skimming across intermediation venues with endogenous participation, a mature mechanism class.

**Status.** `KILL AFTER DEEP DIVE`.

## M4 — Referral-volume public good / hub tipping

**Mechanism.** Every local problem referred to a national hub increases supplier participation, which improves matching for all jurisdictions; each locality ignores some of that hub-thickening benefit.

**Loop.** referral volume → supplier participation → match probability → referral value → referral volume.

**Endogenous margins.** referral; supplier participation.

**Closest threat.** cross-group network effects and platform tipping.

**Fatal attack.** This is a standard platform/network-externality public-good problem with government labels.

**Status.** `KILL`.

## M5 — Bundling/standardization of local challenges

**Mechanism.** Local problems can be kept bespoke or bundled into standardized national challenge categories; bundling thickens the supplier side but reduces local fit.

**Loop.** bundling → supplier market size/search → solution availability → adopter willingness to pool → bundling.

**Endogenous margins.** bundling/standardization; entry/search.

**Closest threat.** search-friction/product-design theory; optimal market segmentation; demand aggregation.

**Fatal attack.** The trade-off is built into standardization versus fit and is already a known search/market-design margin.

**Status.** `KILL`.

## M6 — Supplier multihoming and duplicated application/search costs

**Mechanism.** Suppliers choose whether to join many local intermediaries or one national hub; fragmentation raises duplicated participation costs and changes which suppliers remain active.

**Loop.** fragmentation → supplier multihoming cost → supplier participation → local matching quality → value of fragmentation.

**Closest threat.** platform competition, multihoming/single-homing and integration.

**Fatal attack.** Mature platform-competition mechanism; public assignment is cosmetic.

**Status.** `KILL`.

## M7 — National fallback crowds out local search

**Mechanism.** A national search hub acts as a fallback if local search fails; anticipating fallback, local adopters/intermediaries reduce costly local search or articulation, changing the national pool and fallback value.

**Loop.** fallback quality → local effort → national demand volume/composition → supplier participation → fallback quality.

**Closest threat.** sequential search with outside options; centralized/decentralized venue coexistence.

**Fatal attack.** Requires only a standard outside-option effect to generate crowd-out.

**Status.** `KILL`.

## M8 — Strategic breadth of challenge definitions

**Mechanism.** Local intermediaries broaden challenge definitions to attract more national suppliers, but broader challenges draw supplier attention away from other regions and may lower fit.

**Loop.** challenge breadth → supplier attention/search → rival regions' thickness → local incentive to broaden.

**Closest threat.** platform congestion, competition for attention, search design, and the previously terminated C4-style competition logic.

**Fatal attack.** Becomes ordinary strategic competition for scarce supplier attention plus a breadth parameter.

**Status.** `KILL`.

## M9 — Architecture-induced sorting of adopters and suppliers

**Mechanism.** Simple/well-defined problems self-select into a national platform while ambiguous problems seek local articulation; suppliers enter based on the resulting demand composition, which shifts adopters' venue choices.

**Loop.** architecture → adopter sorting → demand composition → supplier entry → relative venue value → adopter sorting.

**Closest threat.** Idem centralized/decentralized venue choice; Cai–Gautier–Wolthoff market segmentation; platform quality-selection models.

**Fatal attack.** This is endogenous venue sorting/market segmentation unless a new task-assignment response is added.

**Status.** `KILL`.

## M10 — Hierarchical escalation by problem difficulty

**Mechanism.** Local intermediaries handle routine problems and escalate difficult problems to a national specialist layer; the national layer's market thickness affects local escalation thresholds and the distribution of problem difficulty handled at each level.

**Loop.** escalation rule → task composition by level → national thickness/specialization → value of escalation → escalation rule.

**Closest threat.** Garicano-style knowledge hierarchies, Baccara–Lee–Yariv task allocation, market segmentation, and sequential search.

**Fatal attack.** The core allocation of easy problems to local/frontline and hard problems to a higher specialist tier is an established hierarchy/problem-solving mechanism; adding supplier participation does not create a clean new interaction without a second mechanism.

**Status.** `KILL AFTER DEEP DIVE`.

# Mechanism-search conclusion

The candidates span the plausible Q3 design space without reviving C4/C6/C3 or Q19/Q23. Every tractable version maps to a mature parent mechanism: two-sided platform investment, search design/product design, cream-skimming/market segmentation, platform tipping, multihoming, sequential search or knowledge hierarchy. The candidates that look most distinct (M2, M3, M10) become close to those parent classes precisely when reduced to a minimal Stage-4-ready skeleton.
