# Skeptical Referee Report

## Recommendation at this gate

**CONDITIONAL GO to a human-approved Stage 0 only; do not model yet.**

The intersection `industrial policy × cooperative games` is not empty, but most obvious formulations are already occupied. The literature contains endogenous R&D coalitions, government-supported consortia, cost-of-stability subsidies, partition-function coalition formation, cooperative innovation ecosystems, collective conditional adoption subsidies, licensing with strategic outside options, and policy instruments that alter coalition stability. Therefore a paper whose contribution is simply that a government subsidizes a coalition, induces a grand coalition, or uses Shapley/core language should be rejected.

## Strongest surviving idea: C1

The only sufficiently sharp question found is whether **nonseparable coalition-composition policy expands the implementable set relative to additive firm-level industrial policy**. That is potentially a theorem rather than an application.

However, the central weakness is severe: if an individual transfer can itself be conditioned on the same realized consortium composition, then coalition-contingent policy may be a semantic relabeling. The next stage must specify a defensible information/instrument restriction before any functional form is introduced. If the restriction exists only to create the theorem, reject the branch.

## Second idea: C5

Partition-contingent diffusion policy has the strongest formal cooperative-game necessity because a coalition's payoff depends on the rival coalition structure. Yet this is also the most exposed to application-only novelty. Partition-function games and policy effects on coalition stability are mature. A publishable contribution requires an industrial-policy-specific externality and a comparative-static/implementation result not inherited from generic coalition-policy models.

## Third idea: C7

A public portability/open-membership condition is intuitively attractive because an early coalition may privately prefer exclusivity while society values downstream diffusion. But open/exclusive membership in research coalitions and exclusive/nonexclusive technology licensing are direct neighboring theories. Unless the government policy generates a distinct coalition-stability result, this branch will be structurally contained.

## Referee objections that killed the other branches

- `government makes the core nonempty`: cost of stability.
- `government supports a research/innovation consortium`: R&D coalition literature.
- `government requires a participation threshold`: collective conditional subsidy literature.
- `support shifts from innovator to adopter over time`: ordinary R&D/adoption/diffusion incidence unless coalition stability is indispensable.
- `government chooses diverse teams`: team formation/matching and consortium-diversity literature.
- `government insures cooperation`: contracting/risk sharing.
- `public capacity is scarce`: matching/assignment without cooperative necessity.
- `coalition produces learning spillovers`: mature learning/R&D-cooperation mechanisms and risks reopening prior killed branches.

## Institutional interpretation

The Ehime case is useful motivation because FY2026 horizontal deployment in manufacturing/maritime requires an applicant to collaborate with a previous selected supplier and establish an implementation structure before applying. This does not establish a cooperative game empirically. The theory must not claim that Ehime uses core-stabilizing transfers, transferable utility or binding coalition contracts.

## Final referee judgment

There is a **conditional research opportunity**, not a validated paper idea. The correct next step, if authorized, is a Stage-0 primitive/implementability audit centered on C1, with C5 and C7 as ranked backups. No demand system, equilibrium or theorem proof should be started until the C1 individual-vs-coalition instrument distinction survives that audit.
