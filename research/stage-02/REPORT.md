# Stage 2 — Literature Frontier / Novelty Kill Gate

## 1. Executive novelty verdict

- **Canonical verdict:** `GO`
- **Routing/status:** `GO TO MECHANISM SEARCH`
- **Confidence in broad novelty claims:** low
- **Reason for GO:** almost every Stage 0 mechanism is already known in standalone form, but a narrower interaction remains sufficiently unresolved to justify Stage 3 mechanism search.

The audit materially narrows the project. The following are **not** defensible main contributions on their own:

1. learning from other jurisdictions creates experimentation free riding;
2. private discovery is imitated and therefore underprovided;
3. early technology adopters bear adaptation costs that followers partly avoid;
4. decentralization trades local information/heterogeneity against cross-jurisdiction spillovers;
5. centralization versus decentralization changes experimentation incentives;
6. government can act as an early user/test field/lead customer and accelerate supplier commercialization;
7. innovation procurement can leave rents to suppliers, shift deployment risk, bundle R&D and commercialization, or be centralized to enlarge demand;
8. local governments compete for firms or use subsidies strategically.

The surviving research space is therefore **not** “first mover + free rider,” “local information,” “supplier commercialization,” or “public demonstration” separately.

The most defensible unresolved question is whether a new strategic wedge emerges when **a decentralized government finances or attracts a private supplier's field trial, the local adopter/host receives a place-specific early-use payoff, the supplier earns downstream commercialization rents across jurisdictions from evidence generated by that trial, and non-host jurisdictions learn or adopt later**. In this structure, the party choosing/financing the trial, the local beneficiary, the owner of downstream commercial rents, and the beneficiaries of informational spillovers are distinct. No exact model of that joint incidence was located in this audit.

This is only `POTENTIALLY NOVEL`; Stage 3 must generate specific feedback loops and kill any candidate that merely combines known components.

## 3. Literature-family map

### 3.1 Strategic experimentation

Bolton and Harris (1999) makes information from experimentation a public good among multiple agents and derives both free-riding and an encouragement effect. Keller, Rady and Cripps (2005) likewise obtains inefficiently low experimentation from free riding in multi-agent bandits.

**Implication:** information spillover → under-experimentation is exact prior logic, not a contribution.

### 3.2 Private experimentation, copying and appropriability

Acemoglu, Bimpikis and Ozdaglar (2011) studies firms choosing when to experiment when success can be copied. Symmetric equilibrium delays/staggers experimentation relative to the optimum and intellectual-property design can alter incentives. Hausmann and Rodrik (2003) similarly centers social value from discovering profitable activities whose cost information can be imitated, producing too little ex-ante investment/entrepreneurship.

Detragiache (1998) is also unusually close to the “early adopter bears adaptation cost, followers benefit” intuition: firms pay an upfront adaptation cost for foreign technology and early adopters create a positive externality for followers.

**Implication:** first-mover experimentation cost + follower imitation/adaptation benefit is structurally very close prior art.

### 3.3 Federal experimentation

Callander and Harstad (2015) is the strongest direct threat. Heterogeneous districts choose whether and what to experiment with; districts learn from each other; decentralized districts distort experiment choice to discourage free riding; dynamic centralization can create a tournament; the paper studies heterogeneity, transparency, prizes and IPR.

Cai and Treisman (2009) already shows that centralized democracy can generate too much experimentation while decentralization generates too little relative to the social optimum. Cheng and Li (2019) studies local versus national policy experimentation under career concerns and derives conditions under which decentralization generates more learning.

**Implication:** under/over experimentation and local-versus-central experimentation rankings are not novel without a different endogenous margin.

### 3.4 Fiscal federalism and local policy competition

Besley and Coate (2003), Lockwood (2002), and the Agrawal–Hoyt–Wilson (2022) JEL survey establish broad centralization/decentralization trade-offs involving local heterogeneity, spillovers, common financing, political incentives, and strategic competition for mobile activity.

**Implication:** “local information/fit + national spillovers → hybrid finance/local selection” is standard enough that it cannot be the main contribution by itself.

### 3.5 Industrial policy, place-based policy, spillovers and business stealing

Juhász, Lane and Rodrik (2024) places modern industrial policy in a richer governance/instrument context. Fajgelbaum and Gaubert (2025) gives a current theory benchmark for place-based intervention. Bloom, Schankerman and Van Reenen (2013) explicitly separates positive technology spillovers from negative product-market business stealing.

**Implication:** opposing knowledge spillovers and local/private competitive rents are known forces. A sign reversal generated merely by assigning weights to those two effects would be weak.

### 3.6 Public demonstration and field trials

Brown and Hendry (2009) treats government-funded demonstration/field trials as a bridge to commercialization and learning in operational/customer environments. The 2018 “valley of death/technology pork barrel” literature emphasizes underinvestment, public support, learning, failure tolerance, private participation and dissemination. Later work documents diffusion effects of demonstration programs.

**Implication:** “government demonstration produces information and accelerates commercialization” is established.

### 3.7 Public procurement of innovation — major Stage 2 correction

Chiappinelli, Giuffrida and Spagnolo (2025), published in the *International Journal of Industrial Organization*, is a particularly important frontier map. It reviews PPI as industrial policy and explicitly covers government as early user of novel technologies, deployment-risk absorption, lessons for large-scale commercialization, bundling of R&D/commercial phases, buyer decision rights/capability, supplier rents, competition, and procurement centralization/demand scale.

De Chiara and Iossa (2019/2020 CEPR DP) formally compares demand-side procurement with grants under project competition and information constraints. Dai, Li and Chen (2021) formalizes/empirically tests a certification channel from public procurement to supplier finance/innovation. Uyarra et al. (2020), van Winden and Carvalho (2019), and related work document procurement/intermediation, suppliers and local innovation systems.

**Implication:** merely adding a private supplier/adopter to a government experiment is not novel. Supplier commercialization, certification, buyer screening, and procurement design are already central PPI objects.

## 5. Mandatory kill test — pioneer pays, follower waits

Candidate statement:

> A pioneering region bears experimentation cost and failure risk, while later regions learn from the result and selectively adopt successful technologies.

**Decision: `KILL AS NOVELTY CLAIM`.**

Why:

- strategic-experimentation models make information a public good and derive free riding;
- Acemoglu et al. lets firms wait and copy successful experiments;
- Callander–Harstad embeds cross-district learning/free riding directly in a federal system;
- technology-diffusion work has early adopters paying adaptation cost while followers receive positive externalities.

It can remain part of the environment only if Stage 3 identifies a separate strategic interaction.

## 6. Mandatory kill test — policy experiment vs private technology experiment

The distinction is **not sufficient**. PPI and demonstration literatures already study public buyers/sponsors interacting with private suppliers, unproven technologies, testing, commercialization, procurement, certification and diffusion.

The distinction may matter only if the private supplier has an endogenous payoff or choice that changes the equilibrium mechanism. The most promising unresolved examples are:

- supplier self-financing/discount/effort because a local trial creates nationwide downstream commercial rents;
- competition among regions to host a scarce supplier trial/launch site;
- supplier and local government jointly preferring an easy/high-success demonstration because certification/commercialization and political/local credit depend on observed success, even when a harder experiment is more informative;
- region-specific implementation learning that becomes supplier-owned know-how usable in other regions.

These are Stage 3 candidates, not Stage 2 contributions.

## 7. Strongest prior-art threats

### Threat 1 — Callander & Harstad (2015)

If the mechanism reduces to “districts experiment, learn from each other, free ride, and centralization changes incentives,” this project is derivative.

### Threat 2 — Acemoglu, Bimpikis & Ozdaglar (2011)

If it reduces to “firms delay experiments because successes can be copied and appropriation affects timing,” it is derivative.

### Threat 3 — Chiappinelli, Giuffrida & Spagnolo (2025) and PPI theory

If it reduces to “government is an early buyer/test bed that bears deployment risk and helps a private supplier commercialize, with supplier rents and buyer design choices,” it is already inside PPI.

### Threat 4 — fiscal-federalism / local-policy competition

If decentralization matters only because local governments know local needs while central government internalizes spillovers, the result is too standard.

## 8. Surviving white-space statement

The audit did **not** locate an exact model in which all of the following roles are economically distinct and jointly endogenous:

1. a **decentralized public sponsor** chooses support or competes for a private technology field trial;
2. a **local adopter/host** captures place-specific early-use benefits;
3. a **private supplier** owns downstream commercialization rents from evidence/implementation learning generated in that host jurisdiction;
4. **other jurisdictions/adopters** obtain informational or adoption benefits after the trial;
5. the supplier’s national/downstream commercial incentive feeds back into the amount, incidence, location, or design of local public support.

This is classified only as `POTENTIALLY NOVEL / UNRESOLVED`. The phrase “we combine federal experimentation and PPI” would fail the novelty gate. Stage 3 must identify a theorem-generating strategic feedback that disappears if the supplier/downstream market or jurisdictional structure is removed.

## 9. 2024–2026 frontier check

Recent work makes the standard space more crowded rather than less:

- Juhász, Lane and Rodrik (2024): modern industrial policy/governance;
- Fajgelbaum and Gaubert (2025): current place-based theory;
- Chiappinelli, Giuffrida and Spagnolo (2025): IJIO PPI survey;
- 2025–2026 procurement research on spillovers, supplier markets, early supplier involvement, scaling, signaling/screening and market engagement;
- empirical work finds peer/diffusion effects from government innovation procurement/pilot policies.

No retrieved 2024–2026 paper closed the narrow supplier-downstream-rent × decentralized-trial-location interaction, but search failure is not evidence of novelty.

## 10. Skeptical-referee conclusion

The original broad pitch would likely be rejected as a recombination of known literatures. A viable paper requires a much sharper mechanism. The referee therefore kills the broad claim but allows Stage 3 because a small set of mechanism candidates can be formulated where supplier commercialization changes **what local governments strategically do**, rather than merely adding a beneficiary.

## 11. Verdict and next-stage contract

- **Canonical verdict:** `GO`
- **Routing/status:** `GO TO MECHANISM SEARCH`
- **Precise novelty uncertainty:** whether separation of the host government, adopter, supplier/downstream market and follower jurisdictions generates a genuinely new strategic feedback and theorem rather than an additive combination of known externalities.
- **Stage 3 mandate:** generate 8–12 distinct loops, run targeted mini-searches on any newly introduced mechanism family, rank at most TOP 3, identify one preferred minimal candidate if defensible, and stop before formal modeling.
