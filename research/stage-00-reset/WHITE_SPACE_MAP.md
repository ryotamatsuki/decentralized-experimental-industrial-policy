# Stage 0 Reset — White-Space Map

Date: 2026-08-28

This is a targeted mini-audit, not a Stage 2 novelty proof. Search failure is never treated as evidence of novelty. Classification refers to the proposed *question*, not the institutional setting.

## Q2 — Public matchmaking versus side subsidies

**Closest literature:** Edler & Yeow (2016, Research Policy); van Winden & Carvalho (2019, Research Policy); Selviaridis et al. (2025, Journal of Operations Management); Bangert et al. (2026, Journal of Public Procurement); general search/intermediation theory.  
**Known structure:** innovation intermediaries connect demand and supply, build capability, translate needs and facilitate implementation.  
**Missing margin candidate:** endogenous participation on both sides under alternative policy instruments: direct side subsidy versus investment in matching quality.  
**Status:** `POTENTIALLY DISTINCT BUT HIGH SEARCH-THEORY RISK`.  
**Kill condition:** if the intermediary can be represented as an exogenous reduction in matching/search cost and the comparison becomes a standard platform/search subsidy result.

## Q3 — Multilevel innovation intermediation

**Closest literature:** Edler & Yeow (2016); Agrawal, Hoyt & Wilson (2022) local policy choice; public innovation intermediary studies including regional PIIs in Japan; Bangert et al. (2026); general fiscal-federalism assignment literature.  
**Known structure:** local public intermediaries exist; public innovation brokers screen markets and match buyers/suppliers; decentralization literature compares local information and spillovers.  
**Missing margin candidate:** decomposing intermediation into *problem articulation on one side* and *supplier discovery/market thickness on the other*, with the tasks potentially assigned to different government levels.  
**Status:** `POTENTIALLY DISTINCT / PRIOR-ART UNRESOLVED`.  
**Why not yet killed:** recent PPI-intermediation reviews call the field conceptually underdeveloped, while the fiscal-federalism literature generally does not model an endogenous two-sided innovation-intermediation technology.  
**Kill condition:** if the model necessarily reduces to “local has better local information; center has economies of scale,” with no participation/search feedback beyond that standard trade-off.

## Q4 — Public versus private innovation brokers

**Closest literature:** Howells innovation-intermediary tradition; Rossi et al. (2022) public intermediary business models; general search intermediaries/platforms; public provision/private crowd-out; Selviaridis et al. (2025).  
**Known structure:** public and private intermediaries both exist; intermediary capability can substitute for buyer/supplier capability.  
**Missing margin candidate:** public brokerage changes private intermediary entry or suppliers’ private customer-search investment, potentially changing the long-run matching technology.  
**Status:** `POTENTIALLY DISTINCT BUT PUBLIC-PROVISION RISK`.  
**Kill condition:** if public intermediation is simply a subsidized substitute for a private service and crowd-out is a standard public-provision result.

## Q7 — Representative versus capable implementation partner

**Closest literature:** Gechter et al. (2024), selecting experimental sites for external validity; absorptive-capacity/adoption literature; PPI implementation/adaptation studies; C6 negative results on success-versus-information trade-offs.  
**Known structure:** selecting high-outcome sites can be inefficient for external validity; implementation success depends on local capabilities.  
**Missing margin candidate:** the implementation partner is not just a statistical site: partner capability is a productive input jointly used by supplier and adopter and may affect what configuration becomes scalable.  
**Status:** `WEAK SURVIVOR / GECHTER-C6 THREAT`.  
**Kill condition:** if the question becomes “choose high-success site versus representative site,” which is already essentially external-validity selection/C6.

## Q11 — Scarce supplier implementation capacity

**Closest literature:** Chaturvedi (2015), capacity-constrained procurement; capacity allocation/auctions; 2026 IMF structural work showing inelastic scientist supply can make R&D subsidies bid up wages and crowd out private R&D; general subsidy-incidence/crowd-out theory.  
**Known structure:** supply constraints can make procurement allocation inefficient; innovation subsidies can reallocate scarce skilled labor rather than expand R&D.  
**Missing margin candidate:** implementation capacity is supplier-specific applied engineering that determines whether already-existing technology can actually be adopted across heterogeneous customers; local project support may redirect a nationally scarce implementation service.  
**Status:** `WEAK-TO-MEDIUM SURVIVOR`.  
**Kill condition:** if implementation capacity is simply an inelastic factor and the entire result is standard subsidy incidence / wage crowd-out.

## Q19 — Implementation projects as investment in government capability

**Closest literature:** Gur, Macnamara & Saban (2022) sequential procurement with experimental learning; public-sector dynamic-capability literature; Selviaridis & Spring / PPI intermediary capability work; Kähkönen et al. (2025) public buyer dynamic capabilities; state-capacity/industrial-policy literature.  
**Known structure:** buyers can learn supplier type from repeated procurement; public buyers/intermediaries need sensing, seizing, transforming and knowledge-management capabilities.  
**Missing margin candidate:** current implementation projects may produce a durable *policy-production technology*—ability to articulate problems, compare solutions and orchestrate matches—rather than merely information about one supplier. That capability can change future project composition and participation.  
**Status:** `POTENTIALLY DISTINCT / STRONG TOP-CANDIDATE`.  
**Kill condition:** if “capability” can only be represented as generic learning-by-doing or Bayesian learning about supplier quality, with no distinct public decision technology.

## Q22 — Fund projects versus intermediary capability

**Closest literature:** Kähkönen et al. (2025); Rossi et al. (2022); Selviaridis et al. (2023/2025); dynamic-capabilities/public-organization literature; state-capacity investment.  
**Known structure:** public organizations benefit from procurement/intermediation capabilities and intermediaries need resources.  
**Missing margin candidate:** explicit policy allocation between a portfolio’s current projects and the organizational capital that raises the productivity/selection quality of future projects.  
**Status:** `POTENTIALLY DISTINCT BUT CLOSE TO Q19`.  
**Relationship to Q19:** Q19 asks whether projects themselves build capability; Q22 asks whether government should finance capability directly instead of projects. They are not to be combined automatically.  
**Kill condition:** if it reduces to ordinary investment in organizational capital with no innovation-market participation effect.

## Q23 — Pre-procurement market engagement versus downstream subsidy

**Closest literature:** Berkhout et al. (2026, Research Policy); Edler & Yeow (2016); procurement entry/demand-articulation literature; PPI market consultation; Bangert et al. (2026).  
**Known structure:** early market engagement informs suppliers and procurers; formats can have different or adverse effects; interactive learning is important; early engagement is underused.  
**Missing margin candidate:** instrument timing can change *which suppliers enter and which solutions are offered*, whereas a downstream subsidy changes the payoff after the solution set has been formed.  
**Status:** `POTENTIALLY DISTINCT / STRONG TOP-CANDIDATE`.  
**Kill condition:** if engagement is equivalent to a generic information-acquisition technology or an entry subsidy and the eventual innovation result is monotone by assumption.

## Q28 — Adopter-led versus supplier-led discovery

**Closest literature:** PPI demand articulation and challenge-based procurement; innovation contests; directed search; technology scouting/intermediary typologies; Amsterdam Startup-in-Residence.  
**Known structure:** intermediaries can articulate buyer needs, scout markets, broker supplier innovations and support adaptation.  
**Missing margin candidate:** organizational direction of discovery determines endogenous composition: starting from problems may miss latent technologies; starting from technologies may generate locally irrelevant matches.  
**Status:** `POTENTIALLY DISTINCT / MODELABILITY RISK`.  
**Kill condition:** if one search direction is assigned a better distribution by assumption and no strategic entry/search response produces the difference.

## Q30 — Public intermediary sunset

**Closest literature:** Rossi et al. (2022) public intermediary business models; Sala-Vilar et al. (2024) intermediary service/business-model adaptation; general public provision and infant-industry theory; innovation-system transition intermediaries; private search intermediary theory.  
**Known structure:** public intermediaries adapt mandates/business models and depend on resources/legitimacy; some studies explicitly discuss viability when public funding falls.  
**Missing margin candidate:** expected persistence of a subsidized public broker can influence private broker entry and suppliers’ direct-search investment, so public intermediation may be optimal only until the matching market becomes self-sustaining.  
**Status:** `POTENTIALLY DISTINCT / STRONG TOP-CANDIDATE WITH CROWD-OUT RISK`.  
**Kill condition:** if market maturity is exogenous or if the result is simply a textbook temporary infant-industry subsidy.

# Cross-question interface map

| Interface | What the mature literature already covers | Candidate residual question |
|---|---|---|
| Innovation intermediation × search | intermediaries connect complementary actors | endogenous participation and organizational assignment of public search/matching |
| PPI × market engagement | demand articulation and interactive learning | why spend on pre-market engagement rather than downstream transaction subsidy |
| State capacity × procurement | buyer learns and needs capabilities | whether implementation projects endogenously produce future intermediation capability |
| Fiscal federalism × intermediation | local information/scale/spillovers | task decomposition across levels of a two-sided innovation intermediary |
| Public provision × innovation brokerage | public intermediaries exist | endogenous boundary/sunset when private intermediation investment responds |
| Technology adoption × capacity constraints | scarce inputs/capacity matter | whether implementation-specific capacity makes local project subsidies pure reallocation |

# Mini-audit conclusion

The reset has identified a **thin-theory / thick-empirics frontier around public innovation intermediation**. Recent 2025–2026 work documents intermediary functions, knowledge-management routines, market engagement and public-buyer capabilities, while mature economic theory separately covers matching/search, fiscal federalism, state capacity, public provision and procurement.

That does not prove white space. The next full audit must test whether any proposed intermediation question creates a new feedback rather than merely combining those literatures.

The strongest current candidates are Q3, Q19, Q23 and Q30; Q7 and Q11 are retained mainly as adversarial comparators.
