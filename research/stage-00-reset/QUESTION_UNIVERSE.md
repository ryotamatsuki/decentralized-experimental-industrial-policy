# Stage 0 Reset — Question Universe

Date: 2026-08-28

Status: broad divergence only. No item below is a selected mechanism or theorem. C4/C6/C3 and all Stage 2 kills remain binding.

Legend for complexity: `L` = likely 2–3 strategic actors / one main margin; `M` = 3–4 actors or two linked margins; `H` = likely too complex for a first minimal model.

## Family 1 — Who should select / search / match?

### Q1 — Search versus adoption finance
**Question:** When should a subnational government finance the search for existing technologies rather than subsidize adoption after a technology has already been found?  
**Phenomenon:** programmes spend resources identifying technologies/providers before implementation. **Tension:** search may discover new matches, but private adopters/suppliers can also search. **Endogenous margin:** search intensity/source of search finance. **Strategic response:** supplier/adopter participation reacts to public search. **Potential gap:** innovation-policy models often begin with a known project/technology. **Closest literature:** search/intermediation, technology extension, innovation intermediaries. **Fatal risk:** generic search-cost subsidy. **Welfare:** duplicated or missing search. **Why local:** local problem discovery may be geographically embedded. **Generality:** high. **Players:** government/intermediary, adopter, suppliers. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** scouting, challenge calls, matches. **Uninteresting if:** public search is just a constant cost reduction.

### Q2 — Matchmaking versus side subsidies
**Question:** When is a public matchmaker between technology suppliers and adopters more efficient than subsidizing either side of the market?  
**Phenomenon:** programmes actively broker supplier–implementation-field relationships. **Tension:** matching can create trade without paying adoption rents, but intermediary information/capacity is costly. **Margin:** intermediary effort versus side payments. **Response:** supplier/adopter entry changes with expected match quality. **Potential gap:** PPI intermediation is empirically developed; formal policy comparison may be thin. **Closest:** two-sided search/matching, innovation intermediation. **Fatal risk:** standard platform/intermediary model with government label. **Welfare:** match creation, rents, search waste. **Why local:** access to local adopters/problems. **Generality:** high. **Players:** intermediary, suppliers, adopters. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** applications/matches. **Uninteresting if:** intermediation only lowers an exogenous matching cost.

### Q3 — Multilevel innovation intermediation
**Question:** How should technology-search and matching responsibilities be divided between local and national innovation intermediaries when local actors are better positioned to articulate implementation problems but national institutions can access a thicker supplier pool?  
**Phenomenon:** local programmes recruit nationally while working with local implementation fields. **Tension:** geographic proximity to demand versus market thickness on supply. **Margin:** assignment of problem discovery and supplier search across government levels. **Response:** suppliers/adopters choose participation based on reach and match relevance. **Potential gap:** the literature documents intermediary functions but less clearly the optimal vertical assignment of different intermediation tasks. **Closest:** fiscal federalism, search/matching, PPI intermediation. **Fatal risk:** generic local-information-versus-scale trade-off. **Welfare:** match quality, search duplication, participation. **Why local:** demand articulation is local by construction, but local superiority must not be assumed. **Generality:** very high. **Players:** local intermediary, national intermediary, suppliers/adopters. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** referral/matching architecture. **Uninteresting if:** result is mechanically 'local knows local, national has scale.'

### Q4 — Public versus private innovation brokers
**Question:** When does free public innovation intermediation create additional technology matches, and when does it crowd out private brokers or suppliers’ own market-development effort?  
**Phenomenon:** public programmes provide brokering/search at zero or subsidized price. **Tension:** public brokerage reduces frictions but may displace private discovery capacity. **Margin:** public intermediary scope and private intermediation investment/entry. **Response:** private brokers/suppliers alter search investment. **Potential gap:** innovation-intermediary literature mostly asks how intermediaries help, not equilibrium coexistence with private brokerage. **Closest:** public provision/private crowd-out, search intermediaries, business-service markets. **Fatal risk:** standard public provision crowd-out. **Welfare:** match creation versus displaced market infrastructure. **Why local:** local government may be a temporary broker in thin regional markets. **Generality:** high. **Players:** government intermediary, private intermediary/supplier, adopters. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** private broker entry, supplier sales effort. **Uninteresting if:** public service and private service are perfect substitutes by assumption.

### Q5 — Supplier self-selection into demonstration programmes
**Question:** Do government implementation programmes attract projects with systematically weaker private-market prospects, and how should selection rules respond to that endogenous applicant composition?  
**Phenomenon:** suppliers choose whether to apply. **Tension:** support attracts constrained valuable projects but also projects with weak outside options. **Margin:** application/selection. **Response:** supplier application reacts to subsidy/screening. **Potential gap:** little; innovation-finance screening is mature. **Closest:** Takalo–Tanayama; Lach–Neeman–Schankerman. **Fatal risk:** exact adverse selection. **Welfare:** mistargeting. **Why local:** weak. **Generality:** high. **Players:** government, heterogeneous suppliers. **Complexity:** L. **Observable:** applicants/non-applicants. **Uninteresting if:** exactly standard subsidy screening.

### Q6 — Outside-option targeting
**Question:** Should implementation support target suppliers with strong private commercialization prospects or those least able to finance projects privately?  
**Phenomenon:** programmes choose among heterogeneous suppliers. **Tension:** additionality versus expected success. **Margin:** targeting by outside option. **Response:** supplier application/effort. **Potential gap:** likely occupied by optimal innovation finance. **Closest:** R&D subsidy mechanism design. **Fatal risk:** known screening result. **Welfare:** additionality versus selection quality. **Why local:** weak. **Generality:** high. **Players:** government/suppliers. **Complexity:** L. **Observable:** private finance. **Uninteresting if:** one-dimensional screening.

## Family 2 — Which adopter / implementation field?

### Q7 — Representative versus capable pilot partner
**Question:** When should a public technology programme choose the implementation partner most capable of making a project work rather than the partner most representative of future adopters?  
**Phenomenon:** local implementation fields are actively identified. **Tension:** implementation capability raises immediate performance while representativeness raises relevance for scale-up. **Margin:** partner selection. **Response:** suppliers may tailor technology/effort to selected partner; followers respond to resulting evidence/configuration. **Potential gap:** external-validity site selection is close, but implementation capability may be an endogenous production input rather than statistical representativeness. **Closest:** external validity, pilot-site selection, absorptive capacity. **Fatal risk:** C6-like success-versus-information trade-off or Gechter-style site selection. **Welfare:** current project value versus scalable adoption. **Why local:** local sponsor controls field access. **Generality:** high. **Players:** sponsor, supplier, heterogeneous adopters. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** partner capability and later take-up. **Uninteresting if:** capability/representativeness trade-off is assumed directly.

### Q8 — Capability concentration through repeated partner selection
**Question:** Can repeatedly selecting the easiest or most capable implementation partners cause public technology programmes to concentrate implementation capability in a small set of firms and thereby reduce the future adoptability of technology elsewhere?  
**Phenomenon:** capable partners are natural repeated hosts. **Tension:** current execution quality versus diffusion of implementation capability. **Margin:** dynamic partner allocation. **Response:** firms invest in readiness based on future selection chances. **Potential gap:** combines assignment and capability investment. **Closest:** dynamic procurement, learning-by-doing, training allocation. **Fatal risk:** mechanical dynamic increasing returns/agglomeration. **Welfare:** concentration versus broad capability. **Why local:** programme allocates opportunities across local firms. **Generality:** high. **Players:** government, supplier, heterogeneous adopters. **Complexity:** H. **Observable:** repeated hosts and readiness. **Uninteresting if:** capability accumulation is exogenous.

## Family 3 — What should government finance?

### Q9 — Technology purchase versus organizational adaptation
**Question:** When should implementation policy subsidize organizational adaptation and training rather than the technology itself?  
**Phenomenon:** implementation requires workflows/training/integration. **Tension:** complementary investments may be essential but privately chosen. **Margin:** allocation of support across complements. **Response:** supplier/adopter efforts. **Potential gap:** weak. **Closest:** incomplete contracts, technology adoption, team production. **Fatal risk:** Acemoglu–Antràs–Helpman / standard complementarity. **Welfare:** adoption efficiency. **Why local:** weak. **Generality:** high. **Players:** supplier/adopter/government. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** cost composition. **Uninteresting if:** standard moral hazard/complement subsidy.

### Q10 — Implementation subsidy and adopter effort
**Question:** When does public implementation support reduce rather than increase effective adoption because it weakens the adopter’s incentive to make complementary organizational investments?  
**Phenomenon:** public support covers part of implementation. **Tension:** finance relief versus effort crowd-out. **Margin:** adopter effort. **Response:** effort reacts to subsidy. **Potential gap:** low. **Closest:** moral hazard/team production. **Fatal risk:** textbook incentive effect. **Welfare:** effective use. **Why local:** cosmetic. **Generality:** high. **Players:** government/adopter. **Complexity:** L. **Observable:** effort. **Uninteresting if:** standard cost sharing.

### Q11 — Scarce supplier implementation capacity
**Question:** When do project-level technology subsidies merely reallocate scarce supplier engineering/implementation capacity across adopters rather than expand aggregate adoption, and when should policy instead expand that capacity?  
**Phenomenon:** implementation requires scarce technical personnel. **Tension:** project subsidy raises one buyer’s willingness to pay but scarce implementation labor may be diverted from private projects. **Margin:** allocation and creation of supplier implementation capacity. **Response:** supplier allocation/capacity investment and adopter demand. **Potential gap:** technology-policy literature often treats supply response as elastic; current R&D talent crowd-out work raises a direct threat. **Closest:** capacity-constrained procurement, skilled-labor crowd-out, industrial organization of capacity. **Fatal risk:** generic inelastic-factor subsidy incidence. **Welfare:** displacement versus additional adoption. **Why local:** local subsidy can shift national scarce capacity toward its projects. **Generality:** very high. **Players:** supplier, public-supported adopter, private adopters/government. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** queues, staffing, project displacement. **Uninteresting if:** only wage bidding with fixed capacity.

### Q12 — Project subsidy versus implementation-capacity investment
**Question:** Under what conditions should innovation policy finance supplier/adopter implementation capacity rather than subsidize individual adoption projects?  
**Phenomenon:** repeated projects use the same integration/engineering capability. **Tension:** project aid allocates current capacity; capability investment changes future supply. **Margin:** policy instrument choice. **Response:** supplier capacity investment and project selection. **Potential gap:** potentially broader industrial-policy instrument assignment. **Closest:** training subsidies, capacity investment, supply-side industrial policy. **Fatal risk:** standard capital subsidy versus output subsidy. **Welfare:** dynamic capacity creation. **Why local:** local level may not capture benefits of capacity used elsewhere. **Generality:** high. **Players:** government/supplier/adopters. **Complexity:** M/H. **Observable:** workforce/capacity. **Uninteresting if:** conventional investment subsidy result.

## Family 4 — What should government own / make portable?

### Q13 — Performance-data portability
**Question:** When should publicly financed implementation data be made portable to rival suppliers rather than remain with the incumbent supplier?  
**Phenomenon:** trials generate performance/integration data. **Tension:** portability supports future competition but can reduce incumbent investment incentive. **Margin:** data/compatibility rule. **Response:** incumbent investment and future rivals. **Potential gap:** low after 2026 dynamic-procurement compatibility result. **Closest:** compatibility, data rights, dynamic procurement. **Fatal risk:** structurally contained. **Welfare:** investment versus competition. **Why local:** weak. **Generality:** high. **Players:** buyer/incumbent/rival. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** data rights. **Uninteresting if:** identical to compatibility choice.

### Q14 — Ownership of reusable implementation assets
**Question:** Who should own software customizations, integration routines, and other reusable assets created in a publicly supported implementation?  
**Phenomenon:** implementation produces assets beyond technology. **Tension:** ex ante investment incentives versus downstream reuse/competition. **Margin:** ownership. **Response:** investment and reuse. **Potential gap:** low. **Closest:** property rights, IP, procurement data rights. **Fatal risk:** standard ownership/incomplete contracts. **Welfare:** investment versus diffusion. **Why local:** weak. **Generality:** high. **Players:** government/supplier/adopter. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** contracts. **Uninteresting if:** generic property-rights theorem.

### Q15 — Separate purchase of data/evaluation rights
**Question:** Should government purchase rights to implementation evidence separately from the technology/service itself?  
**Phenomenon:** programmes pay for implementation and reporting. **Tension:** bundling may distort price/investment. **Margin:** contract bundling. **Response:** supplier pricing/effort. **Potential gap:** low. **Closest:** procurement contracting/IP. **Fatal risk:** standard multidimensional procurement. **Welfare:** evidence value. **Why local:** cosmetic. **Generality:** high. **Players:** government/supplier. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** contract clauses. **Uninteresting if:** standard bundling.

## Family 5 — When should government stop / scale?

### Q16 — Pilot creates future scale-up entitlement
**Question:** Does financing an initial pilot weaken future termination discipline because success or sunk public investment creates an expectation of scale-up support?  
**Phenomenon:** demonstration may precede diffusion/scale finance. **Tension:** learning option versus inability to commit not to rescue/scale. **Margin:** continuation. **Response:** firm effort/project choice anticipates future support. **Potential gap:** low. **Closest:** soft budget constraints/R&D finance. **Fatal risk:** exact SBC. **Welfare:** inefficient continuation. **Why local:** weak. **Generality:** high. **Players:** government/firm. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** follow-on awards. **Uninteresting if:** Dewatripont–Maskin logic.

### Q17 — Split pilot and scale-up authority
**Question:** When should the authority that finances a technology pilot be institutionally separated from the authority that decides whether to finance scale-up?  
**Phenomenon:** different government levels/instruments can fund stages. **Tension:** informed continuation versus commitment/soft-budget discipline. **Margin:** organizational assignment across stages. **Response:** pilot sponsor and firm anticipate independent scale decision. **Potential gap:** may add a federal assignment dimension to SBC. **Closest:** soft budgets, delegated project evaluation, fiscal federalism. **Fatal risk:** standard separation-of-powers commitment device. **Welfare:** screening/commitment. **Why local:** pilot may be local while scale benefits broader. **Generality:** high. **Players:** pilot authority, scale authority, firm. **Complexity:** M/H. **Observable:** programme architecture. **Uninteresting if:** another principal simply supplies commitment mechanically.

### Q18 — Precommitment to no scale subsidy
**Question:** When does committing ex ante not to subsidize scale-up improve the quality of projects entering a pilot programme?  
**Phenomenon:** entrants may anticipate follow-on support. **Tension:** commitment changes applicant pool. **Margin:** application and continuation. **Response:** supplier entry. **Potential gap:** low. **Closest:** SBC + adverse selection. **Fatal risk:** standard commitment/screening. **Welfare:** project composition. **Why local:** weak. **Generality:** high. **Players:** government/suppliers. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** entrants. **Uninteresting if:** direct SBC corollary.

## Family 6 — Is government capability itself an output?

### Q19 — Implementation projects as state-capacity investment
**Question:** When should a government undertake technology-implementation projects partly to build its own future capability to identify, screen, and govern supplier–adopter matches rather than only to generate current adoption benefits?  
**Phenomenon:** intermediaries learn routines, supplier markets and implementation constraints across projects. **Tension:** a current project can be privately/socially weak yet improve future public decision capability; conversely capability investment may justify excessive project activity. **Margin:** project selection plus endogenous government capability. **Response:** future suppliers/adopters react to improved public screening/intermediation. **Potential gap:** state capacity is prominent, and repeated procurement learns supplier type, but project-generated *intermediation capability* as an endogenous policy asset appears less settled. **Closest:** dynamic capabilities, procurement learning, state capacity, organizational learning. **Fatal risk:** repeated procurement learning or generic learning-by-doing. **Welfare:** current project value versus future policy productivity. **Why local:** local public organizations may repeatedly govern region-specific implementation relationships, but local advantage must emerge rather than be assumed. **Generality:** high. **Players:** government/intermediary, suppliers/adopters over projects. **Complexity:** M/H. **Observable:** changes in screening/match outcomes over programme history. **Uninteresting if:** capability is just a stock increasing mechanically with number of projects.

### Q20 — Experiment with suppliers to learn reliable partners
**Question:** Should government deliberately award early implementation projects to uncertain suppliers to learn which suppliers are reliable for future procurement?  
**Phenomenon:** delivery reveals quality. **Tension:** exploration versus exploitation. **Margin:** supplier choice. **Response:** strategic supplier pricing/participation. **Potential gap:** low. **Closest:** sequential procurement with experimental learning. **Fatal risk:** Gur–Macnamara–Saban directly. **Welfare:** procurement quality. **Why local:** cosmetic. **Generality:** high. **Players:** buyer/suppliers. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** repeated awards. **Uninteresting if:** standard bandit/procurement.

### Q21 — Pool local implementation histories
**Question:** When should local governments pool supplier/implementation performance histories rather than learn independently?  
**Phenomenon:** multiple public buyers accumulate evidence. **Tension:** shared screening value versus participation/privacy/incentive effects. **Margin:** information sharing. **Response:** suppliers alter participation/effort. **Potential gap:** uncertain. **Closest:** information sharing, reputation, procurement. **Fatal risk:** generic information pooling/free riding. **Welfare:** duplicated learning. **Why local:** multijurisdiction setting. **Generality:** high. **Players:** governments/suppliers. **Complexity:** H. **Observable:** shared databases. **Uninteresting if:** information is costless public good.

### Q22 — Fund projects or fund the intermediary?
**Question:** When should government finance individual technology projects and when should it invest instead in an intermediary’s durable capability to discover, translate, match, and manage future projects?  
**Phenomenon:** programme-management/intermediation is itself resource intensive. **Tension:** project money creates direct current output; intermediary capability may raise productivity of a portfolio of future matches. **Margin:** budget allocation between projects and intermediation infrastructure. **Response:** suppliers/adopters participate differently as intermediary quality changes. **Potential gap:** procurement capability literature is growing but mostly empirical/organizational. **Closest:** state capacity investment, dynamic capabilities, innovation intermediaries. **Fatal risk:** generic organizational-capital investment. **Welfare:** portfolio productivity versus current projects. **Why local:** level of durable intermediary capability is a policy assignment question. **Generality:** high. **Players:** government/intermediary, suppliers/adopters. **Complexity:** M/H. **Observable:** overhead/intermediary budgets and match outcomes. **Uninteresting if:** capability simply scales all future matches by a parameter.

## Family 7 — Pre-market engagement and market development

### Q23 — Market engagement versus contract subsidy
**Question:** When should a public buyer spend resources on pre-procurement market engagement and problem articulation rather than subsidize the eventual procurement/adoption contract?  
**Phenomenon:** early market engagement and challenge articulation precede bids. **Tension:** engagement can change who enters and what solutions are offered; contract subsidy mainly changes ex post willingness to transact. **Margin:** pre-market engagement intensity/form versus downstream subsidy. **Response:** supplier entry/innovation and buyer specification adapt. **Potential gap:** 2026 Research Policy finds engagement effects underexplored and heterogeneous; formal welfare/instrument comparison appears promising. **Closest:** procurement entry, demand articulation, information exchange, PPI. **Fatal risk:** standard costly information acquisition or entry subsidy. **Welfare:** variety/innovation versus engagement cost/distortion. **Why local:** local buyer articulates operational problems; national scale may affect supplier reach. **Generality:** very high. **Players:** public buyer/intermediary, potential suppliers. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** engagement format, bids, innovation. **Uninteresting if:** engagement is just an exogenous increase in bid quality.

### Q24 — Public matching and supplier market-development effort
**Question:** Can public challenge platforms reduce suppliers’ incentive to invest in private customer discovery and market development, making publicly brokered projects successful but commercially fragile?  
**Phenomenon:** government brings potential customers to suppliers. **Tension:** matchmaking lowers search cost but can substitute for supplier sales/market discovery capability. **Margin:** public matching intensity and supplier market-development investment. **Response:** supplier invests less/more depending on public lead flow. **Potential gap:** potential interface between innovation intermediation and endogenous search investment. **Closest:** search intermediaries, platforms, public crowd-out. **Fatal risk:** generic free lead/crowd-out model. **Welfare:** short-run matches versus durable commercialization. **Why local:** regional programmes may temporarily generate leads. **Generality:** high. **Players:** public intermediary, supplier, customers. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** private sales activity after programme. **Uninteresting if:** public and private leads are identical substitutes.

### Q25 — Demand aggregation and lock-in
**Question:** When should local governments aggregate adopters around a common technology to overcome minimum-scale barriers, and when does aggregation create inefficient lock-in?  
**Phenomenon:** coordination can create a viable supplier market. **Tension:** minimum efficient scale versus variety/lock-in. **Margin:** standard/technology coordination. **Response:** supplier entry and adopter choice. **Potential gap:** low. **Closest:** network effects, standardization, demand aggregation. **Fatal risk:** standard coordination/lock-in. **Welfare:** entry versus variety. **Why local:** local aggregation. **Generality:** high. **Players:** government/adopters/suppliers. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** common procurement. **Uninteresting if:** standard network model.

## Family 8 — What counts as implementation success?

### Q26 — Sustained use rather than initial adoption
**Question:** Should public technology programmes target sustained post-support use rather than initial adoption, and how does this change supplier/adopter incentives?  
**Phenomenon:** pilot adoption may not persist. **Tension:** quick installation versus durable organizational fit. **Margin:** contract/evaluation horizon. **Response:** effort shifts over time. **Potential gap:** uncertain but likely contract/performance-pay literature. **Closest:** adoption persistence, dynamic moral hazard. **Fatal risk:** generic long-term contracting. **Welfare:** stranded implementation. **Why local:** weak. **Generality:** high. **Players:** sponsor/supplier/adopter. **Complexity:** H. **Observable:** post-support retention. **Uninteresting if:** just longer outcome metric.

### Q27 — Technical success versus commercial viability across stages
**Question:** How should public support be sequenced when a pilot reveals technical feasibility but commercial viability is learned only through later unsubsidized use?  
**Phenomenon:** technical demonstration and viable scale market are distinct. **Tension:** option value versus second financing gap. **Margin:** staged finance/stopping. **Response:** supplier entry/investment. **Potential gap:** low/uncertain. **Closest:** real options, venture staging, demonstration valley-of-death. **Fatal risk:** standard staged finance. **Welfare:** premature scale or abandonment. **Why local:** weak. **Generality:** high. **Players:** sponsor/supplier/market. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** technical vs business KPIs. **Uninteresting if:** two-stage real option.

## Family 9 — Organizational form of discovery

### Q28 — Adopter-led versus supplier-facing challenge search
**Question:** When should a public innovation programme begin from adopter-defined problems and search outward for suppliers, rather than solicit supplier technologies first and search for local use cases afterward?  
**Phenomenon:** programmes can organize discovery from demand or supply. **Tension:** demand-led search improves problem relevance; supplier-led search may reveal technologies adopters do not know to request. **Margin:** direction/organizational form of search. **Response:** supplier and adopter entry, effort and match composition. **Potential gap:** intermediation literature describes demand articulation and technology scouting but may not formalize the organizational-form comparison. **Closest:** directed search, innovation contests, problemistic search, PPI demand articulation. **Fatal risk:** standard two-sided search with renamed direction. **Welfare:** discovery breadth versus relevance. **Why local:** local government may observe/access demand-side problems while supplier pool is national. **Generality:** very high. **Players:** intermediary, suppliers, adopters. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** programme call architecture. **Uninteresting if:** one direction is assumed to draw better projects.

### Q29 — Scarce public evaluation capacity
**Question:** How should an innovation programme allocate scarce expert evaluation and implementation-management capacity across projects when evaluation itself affects supplier participation and outside financing?  
**Phenomenon:** technical evaluation/management time is limited. **Tension:** deep evaluation creates information/certification but fewer projects can be processed. **Margin:** evaluation intensity/allocation. **Response:** project application and private financiers. **Potential gap:** project screening literature is close. **Closest:** costly screening, certification, attention allocation. **Fatal risk:** Takalo-style screening plus capacity constraint. **Welfare:** breadth versus screening quality. **Why local:** limited specialized staff. **Generality:** high. **Players:** evaluator/suppliers/financiers. **Complexity:** M. **Observable:** evaluation resources. **Uninteresting if:** standard optimal screening capacity.

### Q30 — Public intermediary sunset
**Question:** When should a public innovation intermediary withdraw from a technology-adoption market and allow supplier–adopter search to be performed by private brokers or direct market relationships?  
**Phenomenon:** public programmes may create networks that later become self-sustaining. **Tension:** early public brokerage can thicken a market, but continued subsidized intermediation may crowd out private brokerage and direct search. **Margin:** intermediary entry/exit over market maturity. **Response:** private intermediary/supplier/adopter search investment reacts to expected public persistence. **Potential gap:** public innovation intermediary literature studies roles/business models but less the equilibrium boundary and sunset of public brokerage. **Closest:** infant-industry exit, public provision crowd-out, dynamic search intermediaries. **Fatal risk:** generic temporary subsidy / public-enterprise exit. **Welfare:** market formation versus dependence/crowd-out. **Why local:** thin regional markets may justify temporary local brokerage. **Generality:** high. **Players:** public intermediary, private market intermediaries/suppliers/adopters. **Complexity:** M/H. **Observable:** network density and post-program private matching. **Uninteresting if:** market thickness evolves exogenously.

## Hard-test summary before first kill

Questions deliberately vulnerable to one-line mappings are retained here only so that the kill is documented. In particular Q5/Q6 (adverse selection), Q9/Q10 (contracts/moral hazard), Q13–Q15 (property/data/compatibility), Q16/Q18 (soft budgets), Q20 (sequential procurement learning), Q25 (network coordination) and Q27 (real options/staging) are expected to fail early.

The reset’s more promising interface questions are Q3, Q4, Q7, Q11, Q19, Q22, Q23, Q24, Q28 and Q30. Their survival is not asserted here; they require targeted mini-audit.
